STIMMING BEHAVIORS (100 Questions) Visual Stimming
Q1: My child looks at things from the corner of their eyes. What should I do? A: This is visual stimming called peripheral gazing. Provide alternative visual activities like light tables with translucent objects, kaleidoscopes, bubble play, or mirror exploration. Use visual tracking games with moving toys like spinning tops or colorful balls. Create colorful scavenger hunts to encourage purposeful looking.
Q2: My son keeps blinking repeatedly. Is this normal? A: Repetitive blinking can be visual stimming for sensory regulation. Try balloon or bubble play to encourage eye tracking, create patterns with kinetic sand or Play-Doh, use visual tracking games, or provide sensory bottles filled with glitter and water to watch.
Q3: My daughter stares at lights and fans constantly. How can I help? A: Offer controlled visual alternatives like light-up toys, lava lamps, kaleidoscopes, glow sticks, color-changing LED lights, or light tables. Provide different angles and patterns of eye tracking activities and light tracing in slightly dark rooms.
Q4: My child keeps turning lights on and off. What should I do? A: Provide light-up toys with sounds, glow-in-the-dark items, shadow play with flashlights, or color-changing LED lights they can control. These satisfy the visual need in a more appropriate way.
Q5: My son moves his fingers in front of his eyes constantly. Can you help? A: Redirect to bubble blowing to watch bubbles float, kaleidoscope exploration, colorful visual tracking games, water play with floating objects, or pinwheels that spin. These provide dynamic visual stimulation.
Q6: My child stares at spinning objects for hours. Is this a problem? A: This indicates strong visual seeking. Offer fidget spinners, tops, pinwheels, or wheels they can control. Provide eye tracking activities at different angles and light tracing activities in dimmer settings.
Q7: My daughter lines up toys obsessively. What does this mean? A: Lining up provides visual organization and control. Encourage pattern blocks, color sorting games, structured lining activities with purpose, or create art with organized patterns to channel this constructively.
Q8: My child looks at books but doesn't seem to read them. What's happening? A: They may be seeking visual patterns rather than reading. Use high-contrast books, books with repetitive visual elements, interactive books with textures, or engage them in describing pictures to build reading skills.
Q9: My son won't make eye contact. How can I encourage this? A: Don't force eye contact. Try looking at the same object together, practice with mirrors, use preferred activities to naturally encourage shared attention, and remember that lack of eye contact doesn't mean lack of attention.
Q10: My child watches the same video scenes repeatedly. Should I stop this? A: Repetitive watching provides predictability and comfort. Limit duration but allow some repetition. Introduce related videos gradually, discuss what they're watching, or act out favorite scenes together.
Auditory Stimming
Q11: My child hums all the time. What should I do? A: Teach voice modulation through whispering and varying pitches. Introduce structured vocal play through singing songs, rhythmic chanting, clapping games, or rhythm sticks for auditory and tactile engagement.
Q12: My son makes loud vocal sounds constantly. How can I help? A: Provide chewable jewelry or blowing activities like bubbles and pinwheels. Teach voice modulation, offer noise-canceling headphones in overstimulating environments, and create designated "sound break" times.
Q13: My daughter covers her ears frequently. What does this mean? A: This indicates auditory sensitivity. Offer noise-canceling headphones, provide soothing music or white noise, reduce background noise, and create quiet zones where she can retreat when overwhelmed.
Q14: My child taps on everything constantly. Can you help? A: Redirect tapping to soft, non-auditory surfaces like squishy balls. Provide sound-producing fidget toys like clickers or rain sticks, or engage in structured body percussion activities with rhythmic patterns.
Q15: My son repeats everything I say. Is this normal? A: This is echolalia, common in autism and language development. It helps with processing. Use it functionally by modeling useful phrases, create word banks, and provide recording devices to capture speech.
Q16: My child repeats words from TV shows all day. What should I do? A: Use scripts functionally by creating "script frames" where parts can be used appropriately. Introduce call-and-response games, engage in interactive storytelling, and gradually expand to flexible communication.
Q17: My daughter makes clicking sounds with her tongue. How can I redirect this? A: Provide oral motor activities like blowing bubbles, whistles, or harmonicas. Offer chewable jewelry, introduce humming or singing, or use clickers and small instruments for controlled sound-making.
Q18: My child talks very loudly all the time. Can you help? A: Teach volume control through games like "indoor voice/outdoor voice," use visual cue cards showing volume levels, practice whispering games, and provide positive reinforcement for appropriate volume.
Q19: My son listens to the same song on repeat. Should I be concerned? A: Repetitive listening provides predictability and regulation. Allow it but gradually introduce similar songs, create playlists with variety, or use music therapy to expand musical interests.
Q20: My child asks the same questions repeatedly. What should I do? A: This may indicate anxiety or need for predictability. Write down answers, create visual schedules, use social stories about change, and address underlying anxiety with calming strategies.
Tactile Stimming
Q21: My child constantly rubs their skin. How can I help? A: Provide texture exploration with various fabrics, use therapeutic massage with deep pressure, offer sensory bins with rice or sand, provide fidget toys with different textures, or try finger painting.
Q22: My son scratches himself frequently. What should I do? A: Use sensory scratch boards with safe textures, provide therapy putty, create DIY sensory gel pads, offer bubble wrap to pop, or use water play stations for tactile input.
Q23: My daughter touches everything she sees. Can you help? A: This is tactile seeking. Provide sensory bins, texture boards with different materials, fidget toys, weighted items for deep pressure, or create tactile obstacle courses with varied surfaces.
Q24: My child picks at their skin constantly. What can I do? A: Offer fidget jewelry with textured surfaces, worry stones, finger covers for severe picking, keep hands busy with therapy putty, and identify triggers to provide fidgets beforehand.
Q25: My son rubs his fingers together all the time. Is this a problem? A: Provide light to firm pressure activities, roll small textured balls between fingers, offer therapy putty, use fidget spinners, or provide various textured materials to explore.
Q26: My child refuses to wear certain clothes. What's wrong? A: This indicates tactile sensitivity. Remove tags, choose soft fabrics, wash clothes multiple times before wearing, try seamless options, and gradually introduce new textures with positive association.
Q27: My daughter won't walk barefoot on grass. How can I help? A: Start with textured walkways indoors (foam, carpet), gradually introduce textures, make it playful with games, never force, and use desensitization gradually over time.
Q28: My child constantly plays with their hair. Should I stop this? A: Provide textured hair accessories for controlled touching, stress balls requiring two hands, or fidget toys. This is often self-soothing, so provide alternatives rather than just stopping it.
Q29: My son chews on his shirt collars. What can I do? A: Offer chewable necklaces (chewelry), chewy tubes, silicone straws, crunchy snacks, or vibrating oral tools. These provide appropriate oral sensory input without damaging clothes.
Q30: My child smells everything, including inappropriate things. Can you help? A: Create scent bottles with essential oils on cotton balls, provide scented putty or play dough, use aroma diffusers with preferred scents, or offer personal scent lockets or bracelets.
Movement Stimming
Q31: My child rocks back and forth constantly. What should I do? A: Provide rocking chairs, yoga poses for body awareness, weighted blankets, swinging activities, therapy ball chairs, or engage in rhythmic activities like dancing or drumming.
Q32: My son spins in circles until he falls. How can I help? A: Offer controlled spinning with sit-and-spin toys, platform swings, swivel chairs with supervision, dance with spinning movements, or designate safe spinning areas with time limits.
Q33: My daughter jumps constantly. What can I do? A: Provide a mini-trampoline with safety handles, create hopscotch games, set up obstacle courses with jumping elements, use crash pads, or schedule regular jumping breaks.
Q34: My child walks on their toes all the time. Is this normal? A: Practice calf stretches, encourage heel walking, try jumping on trampolines, carry heavy items, play stomping games, use textured walkways, or consult a therapist if it persists beyond age 3.
Q35: My son paces back and forth constantly. Can you help? A: Provide swinging activities, rocking options, balance games, obstacle courses, or structured movement activities. Create movement breaks every 45-60 minutes for regulation.
Q36: My child can't sit still during meals. What should I do? A: Use a therapy ball chair, provide fidget toys under the table, offer chewy foods, give movement breaks before meals, or try a wobble cushion on the chair.
Q37: My daughter crashes into furniture deliberately. Is this dangerous? A: This is proprioceptive seeking. Provide crash pads, body socks, heavy work activities, weighted blankets, or safe crashing opportunities to meet this need without injury.
Q38: My child bangs their head. I'm very worried. What should I do? A: Seek immediate professional evaluation. Provide vibrating pillows, crash pads, weighted blankets, and heavy work routines. Ensure safety with soft helmets or padding while addressing underlying causes.
Q39: My son flaps his hands when excited. Should I stop this? A: Hand flapping is self-regulation. Use wrist weights if excessive, offer stress balls, therapy putty, clapping games, or heavy work activities. Only redirect if it interferes with function or causes harm.
Q40: My child bounces on furniture constantly. How can I redirect this? A: Provide appropriate bouncing options like therapy balls, mini-trampolines, or jumping designated areas. Set clear rules about furniture and offer acceptable alternatives immediately.
Oral Stimming
Q41: My child grinds their teeth constantly. What can I do? A: Offer vibrating oral tools, crunchy/chewy snack rotation (carrots, dried mango), bite blocks or mouth guards, chewy tubes, or chewable necklaces for oral sensory input.
Q42: My son chews on pencils and toys. How can I help? A: Provide chewable pencil toppers, chewelry (chewable jewelry), chewy tubes, or safe chewing alternatives. These satisfy oral sensory needs appropriately.
Q43: My daughter sucks her thumb constantly. She's 7 years old. What should I do? A: Offer alternative oral input like chewable straws, vibrating oral tools, crunchy snacks, or oral motor exercises. Use positive reinforcement and don't shame. Consider evaluation if severe.
Q44: My child licks everything. How can I stop this? A: Provide appropriate oral sensory tools like flavored chewable items, introduce taste-safe activities, offer varied food textures, and use social stories about appropriate licking (food only).
Q45: My son makes chewing motions constantly. What does this mean? A: This indicates oral sensory seeking. Provide gum (if age-appropriate), chewy snacks throughout the day, chewy tubes with resistance, or oral motor exercises like blowing activities.
Q46: My child bites their nails constantly. Can you help? A: Use fidget jewelry, worry stones, finger covers for severe cases, textured nail files as replacement, and identify triggers. Keep fidgets accessible before trigger situations.
Q47: My daughter spits frequently. What should I do? A: This may indicate oral sensory needs or communication frustration. Provide oral motor activities, teach appropriate ways to express feelings, use social stories, and address underlying causes.
Q48: My child drools excessively. Is this related to sensory issues? A: It can indicate low oral awareness. Use oral motor exercises, vibrating tools, practice lip closure activities, offer resistive foods, and consult an SLP for evaluation.
Q49: My son blows raspberries constantly. How can I redirect this? A: Channel into appropriate blowing activities like bubbles, pinwheels, harmonicas, or whistles. These provide similar oral motor input in more acceptable ways.
Q50: My child stuffs too much food in their mouth. What can I do? A: This indicates oral seeking. Work on portion control, offer one piece at a time, provide intense flavors or textures, practice chewing exercises, and supervise meals closely for safety.
AUTISM SPECTRUM (100 Questions) Early Signs & Diagnosis
Q51: My 2-year-old doesn't respond to their name. Should I be concerned? A: Yes, this is an early sign worth evaluating. Contact your pediatrician for a developmental screening. Early intervention is crucial. Meanwhile, use visual cues, reduce background noise, and try multiple modalities.
Q52: My child doesn't point at things. Is this a red flag? A: Lack of pointing by 12-18 months can be an early autism indicator. Encourage pointing through games, model pointing behavior, and seek evaluation for comprehensive developmental assessment.
Q53: My 3-year-old only says a few words. Could this be autism? A: Language delays can indicate autism but also other conditions. Seek speech-language evaluation and developmental screening. Early intervention significantly improves outcomes regardless of diagnosis.
Q54: My child lines up toys instead of playing with them. Is this autism? A: Repetitive play patterns like lining up toys can indicate autism, especially with other signs. This provides visual organization and control. Consult for evaluation while gently introducing varied play.
Q55: My son doesn't play with other children. Should I worry? A: Difficulty with social interaction is a core autism feature. Observe if he shows interest but lacks skills, or truly prefers solitude. Seek evaluation and consider social skills groups.
Q56: My daughter seems to be in her own world. What does this mean? A: This can indicate difficulty with social engagement. Document specific behaviors, seek professional evaluation, and meanwhile try engaging through preferred activities and interests.
Q57: My child had words but stopped talking. What's happening? A: Regression of skills is significant and requires immediate evaluation. This can indicate autism or other conditions. Document what was lost and when. Seek urgent developmental assessment.
Q58: My 18-month-old doesn't wave bye-bye. Is this concerning? A: Lack of social gestures by 18 months warrants evaluation. Model waving, make it fun with songs, but also pursue developmental screening for comprehensive assessment.
Q59: My child doesn't imitate me. Should I be worried? A: Lack of imitation is an early autism indicator. Children typically imitate by 18 months. Seek evaluation while working on imitation through playful activities with preferred items.
Q60: My son screams and has meltdowns frequently. Could this be autism? A: Frequent meltdowns can indicate autism, sensory issues, or communication difficulties. Document triggers, seek evaluation, and meanwhile use visual schedules and calm strategies.
Communication Challenges
Q61: My child doesn't understand simple instructions. How can I help? A: Use visual supports with words, simplify language, give one instruction at a time, ensure attention before speaking, use gestures, and consider speech-language evaluation.
Q62: My son repeats what I say but doesn't answer questions. What's wrong? A: This is echolalia, common in autism. It shows language processing. Model appropriate responses, use visual supports, give response choices, and work with speech therapist.
Q63: My child talks only about dinosaurs. How do I expand topics? A: Use special interests as bridges to new topics. Connect dinosaurs to math, reading, science. Gradually introduce related topics. Set time limits for special interest talk with visual timers.
Q64: My daughter speaks in a monotone voice. Can this change? A: Work with speech therapist on prosody (voice inflection). Use music therapy, practice emotional expressions, record and playback, and provide models of varied intonation.
Q65: My child doesn't understand jokes or sarcasm. How can I teach this? A: Explicitly teach figurative language. Explain jokes, use social stories about sarcasm, watch comedies together and discuss, start with simple puns, and be patient with development.
Q66: My son takes everything literally. Is this autism? A: Literal thinking is common in autism. Be clear and specific in language, explain idioms explicitly, use concrete examples, and work on flexible thinking skills.
Q67: My child asks "what" repeatedly even when they heard me. Why? A: This may indicate processing delays, anxiety, or echolalia. Pause before repeating, rephrase differently, use visual supports, and give processing time.
Q68: My daughter doesn't understand personal space. How can I teach this? A: Use visual markers like hula hoops showing personal space, arm's length rule, social stories about space, practice with family, and provide consistent feedback.
Q69: My child interrupts conversations constantly. What should I do? A: Teach turn-taking with visual supports, use "wait" cards, practice with timers, role-play conversations, and praise appropriate waiting. Consider if underlying anxiety drives interruptions.
Q70: My son doesn't greet people. How can I help? A: Model greetings, practice at home, create greeting scripts, use social stories, start with family members, provide prompts, and praise attempts. Don't force if causing distress.
Social Skills
Q71: My child doesn't have any friends. How can I help? A: Arrange structured playdates with shared interests, join social skills groups, teach specific friendship skills, practice at home, volunteer together, or try activity-based friendships (sports, clubs).
Q72: My son doesn't understand facial expressions. Can he learn this? A: Yes! Use emotion cards, practice with mirrors, watch videos and identify emotions, play emotion charades, read social stories, and work with therapist on emotion recognition.
Q73: My daughter stands too close to people. What should I do? A: Teach arm's length rule, use visual markers for practice, create social stories about personal space, role-play scenarios, and provide immediate gentle feedback in real situations.
Q74: My child doesn't comfort others when they're upset. Is this lack of empathy? A: Autism doesn't mean lack of empathy—it's often difficulty knowing how to express it. Teach specific comforting actions, model empathy, discuss emotions, and practice scenarios.
Q75: My son plays next to children but not with them. Is this a problem? A: Parallel play is developmental. For older children with autism, teach interactive play skills through structured activities, games with clear rules, and adult-facilitated interactions.
Q76: My child doesn't understand turn-taking. How can I teach this? A: Use visual turn-taking cards, timers, games that require turns, practice with preferred activities, make it concrete with "my turn/your turn" markers, and start with short turns.
Q77: My daughter talks at people, not with them. Can you help? A: Teach conversational reciprocity using visual supports showing back-and-forth, practice with scripts, use conversation starter cards, teach question-asking, and work on listening skills.
Q78: My child doesn't understand when someone is annoyed. What should I do? A: Explicitly teach emotion recognition, use video modeling, create emotion charts with levels, practice identifying in real-time, and teach safe responses to different emotions.
Q79: My son doesn't know how to join in play. How can I help? A: Teach specific entry strategies (watch first, ask to join, start similar play nearby), role-play scenarios, provide scripts, practice with siblings, and create social stories about joining play.
Q80: My child prefers adults to peers. Is this concerning? A: This is common in autism—adults are more predictable. Gently encourage peer interaction through structured activities, find peers with similar interests, and don't force but provide opportunities.
Sensory Issues
Q81: My child covers their ears at normal sounds. What's wrong? A: This indicates auditory hypersensitivity. Provide noise-canceling headphones, warn before loud events, reduce background noise, create quiet spaces, and consider occupational therapy evaluation.
Q82: My son refuses to wear certain clothes. How can I help? A: This is tactile sensitivity. Choose seamless, soft fabrics, remove tags, wash multiple times, let them pick clothes, and gradually introduce new textures without forcing.
Q83: My daughter gags at food textures. What should I do? A: This is oral sensory sensitivity. Start with accepted textures, introduce new foods alongside preferred ones, make it playful, never force, and consider feeding therapy if severe.
Q84: My child seeks out strong smells constantly. Is this normal? A: This is olfactory seeking. Provide scent bottles with essential oils, scented putty, aroma diffusers, herb gardens, or scent lockets for appropriate sensory input.
Q85: My son doesn't seem to feel pain normally. Should I worry? A: Altered pain perception occurs in autism. Watch for injuries carefully, teach body awareness, use visual pain scales, and educate caregivers about this difference.
Q86: My child is extremely picky about food. How can I expand their diet? A: Use food chaining (similar foods gradually), make it fun not stressful, offer tiny tastes with no pressure, involve them in cooking, address sensory aspects, and consider feeding therapy.
Q87: My daughter seeks out pressure constantly. What does this mean? A: This is proprioceptive seeking. Provide weighted blankets, tight hugs, body socks, heavy work activities, crash pads, or compression clothing. This need is valid and important.
Q88: My child can't tolerate tags or seams in clothes. What can I do? A: This is tactile defensiveness. Buy tagless clothes, turn seams outside, use seamless socks, try compression clothing, and gradually desensitize through occupational therapy.
Q89: My son freaks out at haircuts. How can I make this easier? A: Practice at home with tools, use social stories about haircuts, find sensory-friendly salons, try distractions like videos, consider starting with just sitting in chair, and go slowly.
Q90: My child seeks out spinning and won't stop. Is this safe? A: This is vestibular seeking. Provide safe spinning options like sit-and-spin toys, swings, or designated spinning times. Monitor for dizziness and teach safe stopping.
Daily Living & Routines
Q91: My child has major meltdowns over small changes. What can I do? A: Use visual schedules, warn about changes in advance, create change stories, teach coping strategies, have transition objects, and build flexibility gradually through small, controlled changes.
Q92: My son won't sleep through the night. How can I help? A: Create consistent bedtime routine, use weighted blankets, ensure dark/quiet room, address sensory issues, use visual sleep schedules, consider melatonin (with doctor), and rule out medical issues.
Q93: My daughter refuses to brush her teeth. What should I do? A: Address sensory issues—try different toothbrushes, flavors, let her choose, use vibrating brushes, make it a game, use social stories, and consider desensitization program.
Q94: My child won't toilet train. I'm at my wit's end. Can you help? A: Be patient—readiness varies. Use visual schedules, social stories, eliminate sensory barriers, create routine, use reinforcement, address fears, and consider if child has necessary prerequisite skills.
Q95: My son eats only 5 foods. How do I expand this? A: Use food chaining, present new foods without pressure, involve in cooking, address sensory properties, create food play activities, and consider feeding therapy for severe restriction.
Q96: My child won't transition between activities. What works? A: Use visual timers, give warnings (5 minutes, 2 minutes), use visual schedules, have transition objects, sing transition songs, and provide choices when possible.
Q97: My daughter screams in public places. How can I prevent this? A: Identify triggers (sensory overload, transitions), prepare with social stories, bring comfort items, noise-canceling headphones, plan shorter trips, have exit strategy, and teach coping skills.
Q98: My child can't get ready for school independently. What should I do? A: Create visual schedule with pictures for each step, break tasks into smaller steps, practice when not rushed, use checklists, and provide reminders without nagging.
Q99: My son has explosive reactions to "no." How can I handle this? A: Prepare for "no" with warnings, offer choices when possible, teach coping strategies, use visual supports, stay calm, validate feelings, and work on flexibility skills.
Q100: My child resists any new activities. How can I introduce new things? A: Use social stories about new activities, show videos, visit location beforehand, start small, have familiar person present, allow observation first, and never force participation.
ADHD (100 Questions) Attention & Focus Issues
Q101: My child can't focus on homework for more than 2 minutes. What should I do? A: Break tasks into 5-10 minute chunks with breaks, remove distractions, use fidget tools, try body double (someone working nearby), provide movement breaks, and consider if work is too hard.
Q102: My son starts tasks but never finishes them. How can I help? A: Break tasks into smaller steps with checkboxes, use timers, provide immediate rewards for completion, reduce task length, work alongside them, and celebrate small completions.
Q103: My daughter is easily distracted by everything. Can you help? A: Create distraction-free workspace, use noise-canceling headphones, face away from windows, remove visual clutter, one task at a time, and teach self-monitoring strategies.
Q104: My child daydreams constantly. Is this ADHD? A: This is inattentive ADHD type. Use verbal and physical prompts, frequent check-ins, break tasks into smaller parts, use timers, incorporate movement, and ensure adequate sleep.
Q105: My son can focus on video games but not schoolwork. Why? A: Video games provide immediate feedback and stimulation that ADHD brains crave. Make schoolwork more engaging, use gamification, provide frequent feedback, and break into smaller goals.
Q106: My child loses everything constantly. What can I do? A: Create specific homes for items with labels, use checklists, establish routines, use tile trackers, limit what they carry, and practice organization skills explicitly.
Q107: My daughter forgets instructions immediately. How can I help? A: Give one instruction at a time, have them repeat back, write them down, use visual reminders, check understanding, and teach self-prompting strategies.
Q108: My child can't listen to a story without wandering off. What should I do? A: Choose shorter books, allow fidgeting while listening, use interactive books, let them move, break into chapters, and make it engaging with voices and questions.
Q109: My son starts homework then plays with pencils. How do I keep him on task? A: Remove extra items, use fidgets that don't distract, work in short bursts, provide movement breaks, work alongside him, and use timers with rewards.
Q110: My child hyperfocuses on interests but can't focus on other things. Why? A: ADHD brains engage with high-interest activities. Use interests as rewards, incorporate interests into other tasks, teach time management for interests, and leverage hyperfocus strategically.
Hyperactivity & Impulsivity
Q111: My child can't sit still during dinner. What should I do? A: Use wobble cushions, allow standing, take movement breaks before meals, keep meals shorter, provide fidgets, and accept some movement as needed.
Q112: My son interrupts constantly. How can I teach him to wait? A: Use wait cards, teach hand-raising at home, practice turn-taking games, role-play, use timers, and provide immediate feedback when he waits successfully.
Q113: My daughter blurts out answers before thinking. Can you help? A: Teach "stop and think" strategies, use visual reminders, practice waiting 5 seconds, role-play scenarios, praise thoughtful responses, and consider if medication needed.
Q114: My child climbs on everything. How can I redirect this energy? A: Provide appropriate climbing opportunities, create obstacle courses, use indoor climbing structures, schedule active play, teach where climbing is okay, and ensure adequate exercise.
Q115: My son talks non-stop. What can I do? A: Schedule "talking times," teach conversation skills, use timers for talking turns, provide alternatives like journaling, ensure he gets listened to, and address if anxiety-driven.
Q116: My child acts without thinking about consequences. How do I teach this? A: Use "if-then" discussions before situations, practice problem-solving, discuss consequences after (not during) incidents, role-play scenarios, and teach stop-and-think strategies.
Q117: My daughter can't wait her turn in games. What should I do? A: Practice with structured turn-taking games, use visual turn cards, start with short waits, use timers, praise waiting, and teach coping strategies for waiting.
Q118: My child runs instead of walks everywhere. Should I stop this? A: Channel energy appropriately—designate running areas, use "walking feet" reminders for inside, ensure adequate exercise time, and accept this is their natural state needing outlets.
Q119: My son touches everything in stores. How can I help? A: Give him a fidget, make a game of hands in pockets, give him a job (pushing cart), use social stories, keep trips short, and provide sensory input before shopping.
Q120: My child takes risks without fear. How do I keep them safe? A: Teach safety explicitly, discuss consequences beforehand, supervise closely, provide appropriate risk-taking opportunities, teach body signals, and use visual safety reminders.
Executive Function Challenges
Q121: My child can't plan ahead for projects. What should I do? A: Break projects into steps with dates, use visual planners, work backward from due date, check in daily, teach planning skills explicitly, and provide templates.
Q122: My son can't organize his backpack or binder. How can I help? A: Use color-coding, simplify systems, establish routines, do weekly cleanouts together, use fewer folders, add pictures to folders, and practice organization regularly.
Q123: My daughter can't estimate how long tasks take. Can you help? A: Practice with timer—guess then measure, discuss before/after, use visual time tools, track actual times, teach benchmarks (5 minutes = one song), and build awareness gradually.
Q124: My child procrastinates on everything. What works? A: Break tasks into tiny steps, use body doubling, start with easiest part, remove barriers to starting, use timers, address perfectionism, and provide immediate rewards for starting.
Q125: My son can't prioritize what to do first. How do I teach this? A: Use priority matrices (important/urgent), make lists together, teach "must do/should do/could do," use visual organizers, and provide decision-making frameworks.
Q126: My child loses track of time constantly. What can I do? A: Use visual timers, analog clocks showing time passing, set alarms, teach time estimation, use time tracking apps, and provide frequent time check-ins.
Q127: My daughter starts tasks but can't finish them. How can I help? A: Ensure tasks are appropriate length, break into steps, work alongside, use checklists, celebrate incremental progress, and address if perfectionism blocking completion.
Q128: My child can't remember multi-step directions. What should I do? A: Give one step at a time, have them repeat back, write them down, use visual checklists, check after each step, and teach memory strategies.
Q129: My son has messy handwriting and disorganized work. Can you help? A: Try different writing tools, use wide-ruled paper, allow typing when possible, teach organization templates, don't penalize messiness if content good, consider OT evaluation.
Q130: My child can't shift between activities easily. What works? A: Give advance warnings, use timers, create transition routines, allow transition time, use visual schedules, and teach flexibility skills explicitly.
School & Learning
Q131: My child's teacher says they're disruptive. What can I do? A: Meet with teacher to create plan, request preferential seating, provide fidgets, allow movement breaks, consider 504 plan or IEP, address sensory needs, and ensure work isn't too hard.
Q132: My son forgets to turn in completed homework. How can I help? A: Create turn-in routine, use checklist, have designated folder, set phone reminder, communicate with teacher about checking, and practice the routine.
Q133: My daughter rushes through work and makes careless mistakes. What should I do? A: Teach "stop and check" strategies, reduce work amount, use self-checking checklists, provide quiet workspace, and reward accuracy over speed.
Q134: My child can't copy from the board. Can you help? A: Request front seating, ask for copies of notes, allow photographing board, use highlighters, check vision, and break copying into smaller chunks.
Q135: My son doesn't write down assignments. What works? A: Use assignment notebook with teacher initials, take photo of board, use online assignment portal, establish routine, and provide reminders.
Q136: My child can't sit through class. What accommodations help? A: Request standing desk, wobble cushion, movement breaks, preferential seating, frequent check-ins, reduced seat time, and fidget tools with teacher approval.
Q137: My daughter tests well but homework is a battle. Why? A: Test anxiety may be lower, or homework lacks structure. Create homework routine, reduce amount, work alongside her, use timers, and make it collaborative.
Q138: My child is smart but failing. What's wrong? A: ADHD affects performance despite intelligence. Seek evaluation, implement accommodations, address executive function challenges, consider medication, and provide organizational support.
Q139: My son gives up immediately when work is hard. How do I help? A: Build frustration tolerance gradually, ensure work is appropriate level, teach problem-solving, break into steps, provide immediate support, and celebrate effort not just success.
Q140: My child's grades vary wildly. What can I do? A: Create consistency with routines, address medication timing, ensure adequate sleep, communicate regularly with teachers, use organizational tools, and track what helps performance.
Emotional Regulation
Q141: My child has emotional meltdowns over small things. What helps? A: Teach coping strategies before meltdowns, identify triggers, ensure adequate sleep/food, provide calming tools, validate feelings, and consider if medication needed.
Q142: My son goes from 0 to 100 instantly. Can you help? A: Teach recognizing early warning signs, practice calming strategies, use zones of regulation, provide sensory input, and work on emotional vocabulary.
Q143: My daughter is very sensitive to criticism. What should I do? A: Use gentle feedback, praise effort, focus on solutions not mistakes, build self-esteem, address perfectionism, and frame feedback positively.
Q144: My child seems anxious all the time. Is this ADHD-related? A: Anxiety often co-occurs with ADHD. Teach anxiety management, ensure treatment addresses both, use calming strategies, consider therapy, and rule out if ADHD medication increasing anxiety.

PRIMITIVE REFLEXES (50 Questions) Understanding Primitive Reflexes
Q301: What are primitive reflexes? A: Primitive reflexes are automatic movement patterns present at birth that should integrate (disappear) as the brain develops. Retained reflexes can affect learning, coordination, and behavior.
Q302: My child's therapist mentioned retained reflexes. What does this mean? A: It means reflexes that should have integrated are still present, potentially affecting development, motor skills, learning, and behavior. Specific therapy can help integrate them.
Q303: How do I know if my child has retained primitive reflexes? A: Signs include poor coordination, difficulty sitting still, sensitivity to stimuli, balance issues, reading/writing difficulties, and attention problems. Seek evaluation from trained professional.
Q304: Can retained reflexes affect learning? A: Yes. They can impact eye tracking for reading, muscle tone for writing, attention, sensory processing, and coordination—all affecting academic performance.
Q305: My child has clumsy movements. Could this be primitive reflexes? A: Possibly. Retained reflexes affect coordination and balance. Seek evaluation to determine if reflexes or other motor issues involved.
Q306: What causes primitive reflexes to not integrate? A: Factors include birth complications, limited tummy time, skipped developmental stages (like crawling), illness, or neurological differences. Often cause is unknown.
Q307: At what age should primitive reflexes be gone? A: Most integrate by 6-12 months, with some persisting until age 3. If present after these ages, integration therapy may help.

NEURODIVERGENCE (150 Questions) Understanding Neurodivergence
Q351: What does neurodivergent mean? A: Neurodivergent describes brains that function differently from typical. Includes autism, ADHD, dyslexia, and other neurological differences. It's a difference, not a deficit.
Q352: Is neurodivergence the same as disability? A: Not necessarily. Neurodivergence describes difference in brain function. Whether it's considered disability depends on individual, supports needed, and perspective. Many neurodivergent people don't identify as disabled.
Q353: My child was just diagnosed as neurodivergent. What now? A: Learn about their specific profile, identify strengths and challenges, access appropriate support, connect with community, focus on accommodations not just intervention, and celebrate their unique wiring.
Q354: Can someone be neurodivergent in multiple ways? A: Yes, many people have multiple neurodivergent traits. For example, ADHD and autism commonly co-occur, as do dyslexia and ADHD. Each combination creates unique profile.
Q355: Is there a cure for neurodivergence? A: No, nor should there be. Neurodivergence is neurological difference, not disease. Focus is on support, accommodation, and helping individuals thrive as they are.
Q356: What's the difference between neurodivergent and neurotypical? A: Neurotypical describes typical brain development and function. Neurodivergent describes different neurological wiring. Neither is better; they're different ways of experiencing and processing the world.
Q357: Should I tell my child they're neurodivergent? A: Yes, using age-appropriate language. Understanding their brain helps build self-awareness, self-advocacy, and self-acceptance. Frame it positively while acknowledging challenges.
Q358: My child is gifted and has ADHD. Is this possible? A: Absolutely. Twice-exceptional (2e) means gifted plus neurodivergence. These children have unique needs requiring support for both strengths and challenges.
Q359: Will my neurodivergent child have a normal life? A: They'll have their own life, which may look different but can absolutely be fulfilling and successful. Many neurodivergent people lead happy, productive lives with appropriate support.
Q360: How do I explain neurodivergence to family members? A: Provide resources, use clear examples, focus on strengths and challenges, explain accommodations, set boundaries about acceptance, and model positive attitude.

Q366: Can neurodivergent people be successful? A: Absolutely! Success looks different for everyone. With appropriate support and accommodations, neurodivergent people excel in many fields and lead fulfilling lives.

Sensory Processing
Q371: My child is overwhelmed by sensory input. What helps? A: Identify triggers, reduce sensory load, provide breaks, create calm spaces, use noise-canceling headphones, teach self-regulation, and work with occupational therapist.
Q372: What are sensory sensitivities? A: Over-responsiveness to sensory input—sounds, lights, textures, smells, or tastes that others tolerate feeling overwhelming. Common in neurodivergent people.
Q373: My child seems to not notice sensory input others do. Why? A: This is under-responsiveness or sensory seeking. They need more intense input to register sensation. Provide appropriate sensory activities to meet this need.
Q374: How do I create a sensory-friendly home? A: Reduce clutter, control lighting, minimize noise, provide quiet spaces, offer sensory tools, consider textures, and involve your child in creating comfort.
Q375: My child has meltdowns from sensory overload. What should I do? A: Identify triggers, prevent overload when possible, teach warning signs, create exit strategies, provide calm-down tools, and support recovery without judgment.
Q376: What's the difference between tantrum and sensory meltdown? A: Tantrums are goal-directed and stop when goal achieved. Meltdowns are nervous system overload, involuntary, and require calming and recovery time.
Q377: My child seeks intense sensory input. Is this a problem? A: Sensory seeking is valid need. Provide appropriate outlets—heavy work, jumping, chewing, etc. Only problem if unsafe or disruptive without appropriate alternatives.
Q378: How can I help my child in overwhelming environments? A: Prepare with social stories, bring comfort items, allow breaks, provide headphones, have exit plan, reduce time in environment, and validate their experience.
Q379: My child's sensory needs seem to change daily. Is this normal? A: Yes, sensory needs fluctuate based on stress, fatigue, health, and environment. Help your child recognize patterns and adjust support accordingly.
Q380: What is a sensory diet? A: Planned sensory activities throughout day to meet individual needs, created with occupational therapist. Helps regulate sensory system and improve function.

A: Children stim for several reasons: self-regulation, sensory processing, emotional regulation, maintaining focus, expressing excitement or anxiety, and managing overstimulation or understimulation.
