SPEECH, LANGUAGE & COMMUNICATION A Comprehensive Clinical Manual for Special Educators and Speech-Language Professionals
Foreword
This clinical manual has been developed to serve as an authoritative, practitioner-facing reference for speech-language pathologists (SLPs), special educators, early intervention specialists, and allied health professionals working with children who present with diverse communication profiles. It is written in the tradition of evidence-based clinical manuscripts, drawing extensively from published American Speech-Language-Hearing Association (ASHA) practice guidelines, peer-reviewed research, and internationally recognized clinical frameworks. The central premise of this manual is that no two children communicate in exactly the same way. A child who produces no sounds at all, a child whose hearing impairment shapes every aspect of language acquisition, a child who can speak fluently in one context but becomes entirely silent in another — each requires a uniquely tailored framework of assessment and intervention. This manual organises that clinical complexity into six discrete communication profiles, each supported by a comprehensive assessment checklist, a detailed intervention taxonomy, and age-stratified activity guidance.
Professionals are encouraged to use this manual not as a rigid protocol but as a living clinical reference — one that is consulted iteratively as a child progresses, regresses, or transitions between profiles over time.
How to Use This Manual
This manuscript is organised into four major parts: Part I — Foundational Concepts: Definitions, developmental milestones, speech sound acquisition, and the ASHA "Big 9" clinical competency domains. Part II — Clinical Profiles: In-depth descriptions of each of the six communication profiles, their assessment frameworks, and their intervention hierarchies. Part III — Profile-Specific Assessment Checklists: Standardised observational checklists for each profile, designed to be completed during structured observation, caregiver interview, or formal assessment sessions. Part IV — Intervention Activities: Age-stratified, evidence-informed activities aligned to each profile and each ASHA domain. Each Profile section follows a consistent structure: (1) Clinical Description, (2) Observable Characteristics, (3) Underlying Factors, (4) Assessment Protocol, (5) Intervention Strategies, (6) Age-Stratified Activities, and (7) Profile-Specific Assessment Checklist. PART I
FOUNDATIONAL CONCEPTS
Chapter 1: Core Definitions in Speech-Language Pathology > 1.1 Speech
Speech is the physical act of using the human voice to express thoughts, ideas, and emotions through spoken language. It involves the precisely coordinated use of the vocal cords, tongue, lips, hard and soft palate, and other articulatory structures to produce and combine vowel and consonant sounds into meaningful words and phrases. Speech is far more than the mechanical production of sounds; it encompasses prosodic elements — tone, pitch, volume, and rhythm — that work together to convey semantic and pragmatic meaning. It is the primary auditory-motor medium through which spoken language is externalised.
Chapter 1: Core Definitions in Speech-Language Pathology > 1.2 Language Development
Language development is the dynamic, lifelong process by which individuals acquire and refine their capacity to understand (receptive language) and use (expressive language) a structured, rule-governed system of communication. This system encompasses spoken, written, and signed modalities, each governed by phonological, morphological, syntactic, semantic, and pragmatic rules. Language is not merely acquired — it is constructed by the child through interaction with their social and linguistic environment, modulated by neurological maturation, cognitive development, and quality of input.
Chapter 1: Core Definitions in Speech-Language Pathology > 1.3 Communication
Communication is the broadest of these three constructs — it is the dynamic, bidirectional exchange of information, intent, emotion, or meaning between two or more participants. Communication encompasses all modalities through which meaning is transmitted: verbal speech, nonverbal gesture, facial expression, body language, and written or symbolic systems. Crucially, communication can occur in the complete absence of formal speech or language, as evidenced by the rich communicative repertoires of prelinguistic infants and individuals using augmentative and alternative communication (AAC) systems.
Chapter 1: Core Definitions in Speech-Language Pathology > 1.4 Conversation
Conversation is a co-constructed, dynamic form of social interaction characterised by reciprocal verbal and nonverbal exchange between two or more participants. It is governed by implicit pragmatic rules regarding turn initiation, duration, topic selection, topic maintenance, repair strategies, and contextual appropriateness. Competent conversation requires the simultaneous deployment of linguistic, cognitive, and social-emotional skills — making it one of the most complex communicative achievements in typical development and a frequent site of difficulty for children with language, social, or pragmatic disorders.

Chapter 3: The ASHA "Big 9" — Clinical Scope of Practice
According to the American Speech-Language-Hearing Association (ASHA), the scope of clinical practice for speech-language pathologists spans nine distinct domains — commonly referred to as the "Big 9." These domains collectively define the breadth of conditions and skills that SLPs are trained to assess and treat. Each domain is elaborated below with clinical context relevant to paediatric practice. PART II THE SIX COMMUNICATION PROFILES
Chapter 3: The ASHA "Big 9" — Clinical Scope of Practice > Chapter 4: The Nonvocal Profile — Clinical Description
Children presenting with a Nonvocal Profile demonstrate minimal to absent voluntary sound production capabilities. This profile encompasses individuals who face significant challenges in producing any intentional vocalisations, often as a result of underlying neurological, structural, or motor-based conditions. These children typically attempt communication through non-vocal means — gesture, eye gaze, facial expression, or object manipulation — and may show preserved cognitive and receptive language abilities that are markedly discrepant from their expressive output. The clinical complexity of this profile lies in its heterogeneity: a child with a structural anomaly such as cleft palate may present very differently from a child with childhood apraxia of speech (CAS) or severe cerebral palsy, even though all three may appear nonvocal in functional contexts. Thorough differential diagnosis is therefore essential prior to selecting intervention targets.
Chapter 3: The ASHA "Big 9" — Clinical Scope of Practice > Chapter 4: The Nonvocal Profile — Clinical Description > 4.1 Clinical Characteristics
Absent or extremely limited voluntary sound production across multiple sessions and contexts Communication attempts made primarily through nonverbal means (gaze, gesture, pointing, crying) Possible difficulties with respiratory coordination and breath support for phonation Oral-motor dysfunction: reduced lip seal, limited tongue mobility, jaw instability Possible feeding and swallowing difficulties (co-occurring dysphagia) Preserved or relatively intact receptive language skills compared to expressive output
Chapter 3: The ASHA "Big 9" — Clinical Scope of Practice > Chapter 4: The Nonvocal Profile — Clinical Description > 4.3 Assessment Protocol
Assessment of the Nonvocal child must be comprehensive, multidisciplinary, and conducted across multiple sessions. A single-session assessment is insufficient to capture the full range of communicative behaviours. The following components are essential: Complete oral-motor examination: lip mobility, tongue range of motion, jaw stability, palatal structure integrity Swallowing and feeding evaluation: observation of suck-swallow-breathe coordination in infants; trial food textures in older children Respiratory function assessment: observation of breathing patterns, posture effects on respiration, breath support capacity Neurological screening: referral for neuroimaging or neurological consultation when aetiology is unclear AAC feature matching: systematic trial of low-tech to high-tech AAC systems to identify optimal access method Caregiver interview: inventory of current communicative functions, preferred contexts, and communication partners
Chapter 3: The ASHA "Big 9" — Clinical Scope of Practice > Chapter 4: The Nonvocal Profile — Clinical Description > 4.4 Intervention: Augmentative and Alternative Communication (AAC)
AAC is the cornerstone intervention for the Nonvocal child. The ASHA position statement on AAC (2004, updated 2021) affirms that AAC should be introduced as early as possible, without prerequisite cognitive or language thresholds ("prerequisite-free" approach). Early AAC provision does not impede the development of natural speech; converging evidence suggests it supports it (Romski & Sevcik, 2005).
Chapter 3: The ASHA "Big 9" — Clinical Scope of Practice > Chapter 4: The Nonvocal Profile — Clinical Description > 4.5 Intervention: Oral-Motor and Respiratory Training
Facial massage and passive stretching of articulators (with medical clearance) Lip strengthening exercises: lip seals, resistance exercises with a Lip Spreader or straw Tongue mobility exercises: tongue protrusion, lateralisation, elevation Jaw stability activities: biting on chewy tubes, jaw-opening resistance exercises Diaphragmatic breathing practice: supported positioning for respiration (e.g., sidelying, prone in therapy) Bubble blowing, windmill toys, and sustained vowel phonation for respiratory-phonatory coordination Sensory stimulation: oral vibration, deep pressure to perioral musculature to increase awareness
Chapter 3: The ASHA "Big 9" — Clinical Scope of Practice > Chapter 4: The Nonvocal Profile — Clinical Description > 4.6 Parent and Caregiver Training
Parent-implemented intervention is critical for generalisation. Caregivers should be trained in: (a) how to use and model the AAC system in daily routines (aided language stimulation / partner augmented input), (b) how to follow the child's communicative lead, (c) how to respond to nonverbal communicative attempts contingently and consistently, and (d) how to carry out prescribed oral-motor exercises safely at home.

Chapter 3: The ASHA "Big 9" — Clinical Scope of Practice > Chapter 5: The Auditory Profile — Clinical Description
The Auditory Profile characterises children whose speech and language limitations primarily arise from hearing impairment. The degree, type, configuration, and timing of onset of the hearing loss, as well as the timing and quality of intervention (including audiological management and speech-language therapy), are the principal determinants of communicative outcome. Children in this profile occupy a broad clinical spectrum: from those with mild conductive losses that cause fluctuating hearing and inconsistent phoneme perception, to those with profound bilateral sensorineural hearing loss whose entire language system is built through visual and tactile-kinaesthetic channels. A fundamental principle of clinical work with this population is the primacy of early identification and early intervention. Every month of auditory deprivation during the sensitive period of language acquisition (birth to 3.5 years) carries cumulative consequences for phonological, lexical, syntactic, and pragmatic development. Conversely, children who receive appropriate amplification and high-quality auditory-verbal therapy in the first year of life can achieve language outcomes that closely parallel those of hearing peers.
Chapter 3: The ASHA "Big 9" — Clinical Scope of Practice > Chapter 5: The Auditory Profile — Clinical Description > 5.1 Clinical Characteristics
Limited detection of environmental sounds or speech at conversational levels Delayed or absent speech development relative to hearing peers Voice quality differences: elevated pitch, reduced prosodic variation, hypernasality in profound loss Difficulty with speech sound discrimination, particularly for high-frequency fricatives (/s/, /sh/, /f/, /th/) Inconsistent responses to auditory input — better responses in quiet, worse in background noise May rely heavily on visual cues (lip reading, facial expression, gesture) for comprehension
Chapter 3: The ASHA "Big 9" — Clinical Scope of Practice > Chapter 5: The Auditory Profile — Clinical Description > 5.2 Assessment Components
Comprehensive audiological evaluation: pure-tone audiometry, tympanometry, otoacoustic emissions (OAEs), auditory brainstem response (ABR) Speech perception assessment: phoneme identification, word recognition, sentence recognition in quiet and noise Language evaluation: standardised receptive and expressive language measures appropriate for hearing status Functional auditory skills inventory: Infant-Toddler Meaningful Auditory Integration Scale (IT-MAIS), MAIS, PEACH Hearing technology evaluation: current hearing aid or cochlear implant fitting verification
Chapter 3: The ASHA "Big 9" — Clinical Scope of Practice > Chapter 5: The Auditory Profile — Clinical Description > 5.3 Intervention Strategies
Auditory-verbal therapy (AVT) is the highest-evidence intervention for children with hearing loss who have been fitted with appropriate amplification. The AVT approach, described in the Principles of Auditory-Verbal Practice (Estabrooks, 2012), promotes the prioritisation of listening as the primary channel for speech and language learning, in partnership with parents who are trained as the child's primary facilitators.

Chapter 3: The ASHA "Big 9" — Clinical Scope of Practice > Chapter 6: The Phonological Profile — Clinical Description
Children with a Phonological Profile demonstrate the physical capacity to produce a wide range of speech sounds, yet exhibit consistent or inconsistent error patterns that compromise overall intelligibility. These errors are not primarily the result of oral-motor dysfunction (as in dysarthria) or motor planning deficits (as in CAS), but rather reflect difficulties in the mental representation and organisation of the phonological system — the abstract, rule-governed sound system of language. Phonological disorders are the most common speech-language difficulty in preschool-aged children, with prevalence estimates ranging from 8–15% in children aged 3–6 years (Law et al., 2000). Phonological errors are described in terms of phonological processes — systematic simplification rules that children apply to adult target words. While all children apply phonological processes during typical development, children with phonological disorders continue to use these processes beyond the age at which they normally resolve, or they may apply atypical processes not seen in typical development.

Chapter 3: The ASHA "Big 9" — Clinical Scope of Practice > Chapter 7: The Pre-Linguistic Profile — Clinical Description
Children with a Pre-Linguistic Profile have achieved some degree of vocal communication and may produce single words or a restricted set of word approximations, but have not yet attained age-appropriate language development. These children often demonstrate a marked discrepancy between their receptive language abilities — which may approach or fall within age-level norms — and their expressive language output, which is characterised by limited vocabulary, absent or minimal word combinations, and heavy reliance on gesture and nonverbal communication to express communicative intent. This profile most commonly presents in toddlers and preschool-aged children (18–42 months), and may reflect a number of underlying conditions including late language emergence (LLE), developmental language disorder (DLD), autism spectrum disorder (ASD) with functional speech, or global developmental delay. The differential diagnostic task is critical: a child who is a "late talker" with intact comprehension, symbolic play, and gesture has a meaningfully different prognosis from a child whose pre-linguistic profile is embedded within broader neurodevelopmental concerns.
Chapter 3: The ASHA "Big 9" — Clinical Scope of Practice > Chapter 7: The Pre-Linguistic Profile — Clinical Description > 7.1 Clinical Characteristics
Single word utterances used functionally but not combined into phrases Restricted vocabulary range — typically fewer words than chronological age norms suggest Heavy reliance on gesture (pointing, reaching, showing, giving) to supplement or replace speech Reduced sentence formation — absence of canonical two-word combinations beyond expected age Grammatical morphology absent or emerging — no regular past tense, plural, or possessive markers Comprehension typically exceeds expression; child may follow multi-step instructions they cannot produce
Chapter 3: The ASHA "Big 9" — Clinical Scope of Practice > Chapter 7: The Pre-Linguistic Profile — Clinical Description > 7.2 Intervention: Language Stimulation Framework
The theoretical foundation for pre-linguistic intervention is social-interactionist theory (Vygotsky, 1978; Bruner, 1983), which holds that language is acquired through scaffolded interaction within the zone of proximal development. Intervention therefore prioritises creating optimal learning opportunities within meaningful social contexts, rather than drilling isolated responses.

Chapter 3: The ASHA "Big 9" — Clinical Scope of Practice > Chapter 8: The Selective Profile — Clinical Description
Children with a Selective Profile demonstrate the physical and linguistic capacity for speech and communication but show marked variation in their willingness or ability to communicate across different settings, situations, or with different communication partners. This profile most commonly manifests in two broad clinical presentations: (1) selective mutism (SM), in which a child who speaks typically at home is consistently unable to speak in specific social settings such as school, and (2) autism spectrum disorder (ASD) with context-dependent communication, in which communicative output varies significantly based on environmental demands, arousal state, sensory factors, or relationship familiarity. The critical distinguishing feature of this profile is that the communication difficulty is primarily situational rather than structural or linguistic. These children possess the tools of communication but encounter significant barriers — typically anxiety, sensory dysregulation, social-cognitive differences, or some combination — that inhibit or suppress communication in specific contexts. Assessment and intervention must therefore address both the communication skill itself and the environmental and psychological factors that mediate its expression.
Chapter 3: The ASHA "Big 9" — Clinical Scope of Practice > Chapter 8: The Selective Profile — Clinical Description > 8.1 Clinical Characteristics
Consistent speech and language in comfortable, familiar contexts (e.g., home, with close family members) Consistent absence or marked reduction of speech in specific contexts (e.g., school, with unfamiliar adults) Non-verbal communication (nodding, pointing, writing) may be preserved in selective settings Anxiety symptoms observable in demanding communication contexts: freezing, avoidance, physiological arousal Social interaction challenges that vary by context and communication partner Sensory sensitivities that may modulate communicative output (noise, lighting, crowding)
Chapter 3: The ASHA "Big 9" — Clinical Scope of Practice > Chapter 8: The Selective Profile — Clinical Description > 8.3 Graduated Exposure Framework
The gold-standard intervention for selective mutism is Behavioural Stimulus Fading combined with Graduated Exposure (Cohan et al., 2008; Bergman, 2013). The underlying principle is to begin with the least-demanding communicative act in the anxiety-provoking context and systematically increase demands as the child builds tolerance and success.

Chapter 3: The ASHA "Big 9" — Clinical Scope of Practice > Chapter 9: The Responsive Profile — Clinical Description
Children with a Responsive Profile possess adequate vocabulary, intelligible speech, and the structural capacity for language production, yet demonstrate significant difficulties with the social and pragmatic dimensions of communication. These children respond to direct questions and participate in scaffolded interactions, but struggle to initiate conversations independently, maintain topics across multiple turns, adapt their communication to different partners or contexts, and engage in the reciprocal, dynamic exchange that characterises competent conversation. This profile is frequently associated with pragmatic language impairment (PLI), autism spectrum disorder (ASD) — particularly at higher levels of language ability, developmental language disorder (DLD) with pragmatic overlay, and social communication disorder (SCD) as defined by DSM-5 (2013). The clinical mandate is to move the child from a communication style that is reactive and bounded to one that is generative, flexible, and socially responsive across diverse partners and contexts.
Chapter 3: The ASHA "Big 9" — Clinical Scope of Practice > Chapter 9: The Responsive Profile — Clinical Description > 9.1 Clinical Characteristics
Responds to direct questions but rarely initiates conversation or topic spontaneously Provides brief, minimal responses — does not elaborate, expand, or contribute new information Limited topic maintenance — responses stay on topic for one or two turns then trail off or change topic Reduced social reciprocity — does not demonstrate curiosity about the partner's perspective or experience Difficulties with presupposition — does not modulate how much background information to provide to different listeners May have difficulty with indirect language, inference, humour, sarcasm, or non-literal expressions
Chapter 3: The ASHA "Big 9" — Clinical Scope of Practice > Chapter 9: The Responsive Profile — Clinical Description > 9.3 Social Thinking and Conversational Coaching
Michelle Garcia Winner's Social Thinking framework (2007) provides a comprehensive clinical structure for working with children who have social-communicative learning differences. Key constructs include the distinction between expected and unexpected social behaviours, the four steps of communication (think about the person, establish physical presence, use eyes to think about others, use language to communicate), and the concept of "thinking about others' thoughts."

Chapter 10: Data Collection, Progress Monitoring, and Family Integration > 10.1 Establishing Baseline Measures
No intervention should commence without a documented baseline. Baseline data serve as the comparison standard against which progress is measured. For each active treatment target, the clinician should collect a minimum of three baseline data points across three separate sessions to confirm the target is in deficit and to establish measurement stability. Baseline data may be collected through standardised assessments, curriculum-based measurement, or structured probe tasks designed to elicit the target behaviour.
Chapter 10: Data Collection, Progress Monitoring, and Family Integration > 10.2 Progress Review Schedule
Weekly: Administer structured probes for each active target; graph data; visual inspection of trend Monthly: Review trend data; adjust treatment intensity or approach if fewer than 3 consecutive data points show improvement Quarterly: Re-administer standardised measures on key domains; update goals; conference with caregivers and educational team Annually: Full re-evaluation; update eligibility; project next 12 months of intervention targets and timelines
Chapter 10: Data Collection, Progress Monitoring, and Family Integration > 10.3 Family Integration Protocol
The research evidence is unambiguous: family-implemented intervention significantly accelerates outcomes across all communication profiles (Roberts & Kaiser, 2011; Kashinath et al., 2006). The role of the SLP or special educator is not merely to treat the child, but to transfer therapeutic knowledge and strategies to the individuals who spend the most time with the child. The following components constitute best practice for family integration: Strategy Demonstration: Clinician models the target strategy during a session while caregiver observes; provides live commentary on the rationale and expected child response Guided Practice: Caregiver implements the strategy under clinician supervision; clinician provides immediate, constructive feedback Feedback Session: Clinician and caregiver review video-recorded home practice; problem-solve challenges; celebrate progress Home Programming Package: Written instructions, visual prompt cards, target word lists, and data recording sheets provided at each session
Caregiver Goal-Setting: Caregiver participates actively in goal selection; goals reflect family priorities and daily routine contexts
Chapter 10: Data Collection, Progress Monitoring, and Family Integration > 10.4 Collaborative and School-Based Implementation
Speech-language intervention conducted in isolation from the child's educational environment is insufficient for meaningful generalisation. Best-practice models position the SLP as a collaborative consultant to the educational team, with intervention occurring across push-in (in-classroom) and pull-out (individual/small group) formats. Key collaborative practices include: Individual Education Plan (IEP) development: SLP goals are written in functional, measurable terms and are embedded within the child's academic and social participation goals Classroom consultation: SLP advises teachers on environmental modifications (seating, visual supports, acoustic conditions) that support the child's communication profile Teacher coaching: SLP models strategies within the classroom and coaches teaching staff to implement them during instruction Peer-mediated intervention: Typically developing peers are trained to use targeted communication strategies during natural social interactions
Chapter 10: Data Collection, Progress Monitoring, and Family Integration > 10.5 Ethical and Cultural Considerations
All clinical practice described in this manual must be implemented within a framework of cultural humility, family-centred care, and ethical accountability. SLPs and special educators are obligated to: (a) obtain informed consent for assessment and intervention, (b) ensure that assessment tools are culturally and linguistically appropriate for each child's background, (c) distinguish language difference from language disorder, (d) provide services in the language of the family where feasible, and (e) collaborate with cultural and linguistic community liaisons when working with families from non-dominant backgrounds.
